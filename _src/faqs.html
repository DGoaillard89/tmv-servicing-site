TMV basics

What is a thermostatic mixing valve (TMV)?

A TMV is a valve that blends hot and cold water to a set temperature before it reaches a tap, shower or bath. A temperature-sensitive element inside reacts when the supplies change, so the water at the outlet stays steady. If the cold supply fails, a good TMV shuts the flow down rather than sending through hot water. HSE describes them in HSG274 Part 2 as blending to a safe temperature, typically between 38°C and 46°C depending on the outlet.

Why not just turn the hot water down instead?

Because cooler hot water lets Legionella grow. HSE guidance expects hot water to be stored at 60°C and to reach outlets at 50°C or more within a minute (55°C in healthcare premises). That's hot enough to scald, so a TMV at the outlet lets you keep the system hot for Legionella control and still deliver safe water where people wash.

Where should a TMV be fitted?

As close to the outlet as you can get it. HSG274 Part 2 says TMVs should be as close to the point of use as possible to keep the run of blended water short, and that mixing in the tap itself is preferable where practicable. It also needs to be somewhere an engineer can reach for testing, which matters more than people expect when panels get tiled in.

What's the difference between TMV2 and TMV3?

TMV2 is the certification scheme for domestic-type installations, based on BS EN 1111 (high pressure) and BS EN 1287 (low pressure). TMV3 is the healthcare scheme, based on the NHS performance specification D 08, and its valves are pre-set and fail-safe so users can't override them. HSE says TMV3 is the standard in healthcare and where vulnerable people use the water. Our TMV2 vs TMV3 guide goes into the detail.

Can one TMV serve several taps?

It can, but it's best avoided. HSG274 Part 2 notes that the Legionella risk can go up where one TMV serves multiple outlets, because there's more blended water sitting in pipework, and some of those outlets may be little used. Healthcare guidance in HTM 04-01 Part B also says TMVs shouldn't be fitted in series with mixing taps, so a TMV feeding a thermostatic tap is a no.

The law and the guidance

Is there a law that says I must fit TMVs?

No single law says "fit TMVs", but the duty to control scalding risk is a legal one. The Health and Safety at Work etc. Act 1974 and the Management Regulations require you to assess and control the risk. HSE's HSIS6 says engineering controls such as TMVs should stop water hotter than 44°C reaching outlets where vulnerable people could be fully immersed. New homes have their own rule under Building Regulations Part G.

What does HTM 04-01 say about TMVs?

HTM 04-01 is the Department of Health's guidance on safe water in healthcare premises, in three parts plus supplements. Part B (operational management) says TMVs should be fitted only where a scalding risk assessment shows a need, that their settings and operation must be checked regularly in line with Chapter 11 of the D 08 supplement, and that strainers should be cleaned, descaled and disinfected annually or as the risk assessment sets. Our HTM 04-01 and D 08 guide walks through it.

What is the D 08 specification?

D 08 is the HTM 04-01 supplement "Performance specification D 08: thermostatic mixing valves (healthcare premises)", currently the 2017 edition. Most of it is the laboratory testing a valve must pass to earn TMV3 certification. The part that matters on site is Chapter 11, which sets out how valves are commissioned and tested in service, and Annex F, which suggests how often to test.

Does HTM 04-01 apply to care homes, schools or housing?

It's written for healthcare premises, so it isn't aimed at care homes, schools or housing. In practice many care providers, housing associations and local authorities use the D 08 test method and the TMV3 standard as their benchmark, because it's the clearest method around. HSE's health and social care guidance also points care settings to TMV3 for new and replacement valves.

Where do ACOP L8 and HSG274 come in?

ACOP L8 is HSE's Approved Code of Practice for controlling Legionella in water systems, and HSG274 is the technical guidance that goes with it, with Part 2 covering hot and cold water. HSG274 Part 2 covers when TMVs are needed, where to fit them, and says their strainers should be inspected, cleaned, descaled and disinfected annually or at a frequency set by the risk assessment. Your TMV records belong in your L8 written scheme.

What does HSE say about scalding in care settings?

HSE's guidance sheet HSIS6, Managing the risk from hot water and surfaces in health and social care, says there is an increased risk of serious injury or death where bathing or showering water is above 44°C. It expects TMVs to be fitted as close as possible to the outlet, Type 3 valves to be used for new and replacement installations, and regular safety testing. The HSE scalding and burning page has more.

What does Building Regulations Part G say about bath temperatures?

Approved Document G limits the hot water supplied to a bath in a new home to 48°C, usually with a TMV or similar device. That's a minimum standard for dwellings and higher than the healthcare figures, so a care home or supported living scheme would normally set baths lower based on its own risk assessment.

Temperatures

What temperature should a TMV be set to?

For healthcare-type settings, D 08 gives maximum mixed water temperatures of 38°C for a bidet, 41°C for a shower, 41°C for a washbasin (washing under running water) and 44°C for a bath fill. A 46°C bath fill is allowed only where the bather is always supervised by a competent person, such as a nurse or carer. Your risk assessment can set lower. See our compliance guide for the full table.

Can a TMV be set lower than those maximums?

Yes. D 08 says a lower temperature can be set after a risk assessment, for example for residents with reduced sensitivity to heat. Just don't go so low that the valve can't control properly, and check the manufacturer's minimum temperature differential. Water that's lukewarm also tends to get people asking for the TMV to be "turned up", so agree the setting with the people who use it.

Why do the hot and cold supplies matter to a TMV?

A TMV can only blend what it's given. D 08 sets normal-use supplies of hot at 55°C or more and cold at 20°C or less. It also gives pressure ranges: 1 to 5 bar flowing for high pressure valves, 0.2 to 1 bar for low pressure. If the hot is too cool, or the gap between hot and mixed is too small, the valve can't hold its setting. That's why we record supply temperatures and pressures at every test.

Testing and servicing

How often should TMVs be tested?

Annex F of D 08 suggests testing a new valve at 6 to 8 weeks and 12 to 15 weeks after commissioning. If the temperature has barely moved (about 1°C or less), the next test can wait until 24 to 28 weeks. If it drifted by more than 2°C, the valve needs service work and another test at 18 to 21 weeks. After that, set the interval to what the valve has shown it can hold. Strainers are serviced at least annually. More in how often should TMVs be serviced?

What happens during an in-service test?

Following Chapter 11 of D 08, we record the hot and cold supply temperatures and pressures, then the mixed temperature at each outlet at full flow. Next comes the fail-safe check with the cold supply isolated. We restore the cold and confirm the temperature has come back to where it was. We note whether the strainers and check valves are clean, and record the final setting and the next test date. Our servicing page covers the rest of the visit.

What is the fail-safe test, and what counts as a pass?

We isolate the cold supply and watch the outlet. Under D 08, if water is still flowing after 5 seconds we collect it for 60 seconds, and more than 120 ml means the valve needs recommissioning or service work. If it passes, we restore the cold and, after 15 seconds, check the mixed temperature is within 2°C of the earlier reading. If you've had a failure, read your TMV failed its fail-safe test.

What happens to the strainers and check valves?

They get inspected, cleaned, descaled and disinfected, because a blocked strainer starves the valve and throws the blend off. HTM 04-01 Part B and HSG274 Part 2 both put this at annually, or at a frequency set by the risk assessment, taking account of the manufacturer's recommendations. In hard water areas we often find strainers are the main reason a valve has drifted.

Why do TMVs drift out of setting?

The usual culprits are limescale on the thermostatic element, debris in the strainers, worn seals or cartridges, and changes in supply temperature or pressure. D 08 points out that varying supply conditions and water quality can change how a valve performs, which is why test intervals are based on each valve's track record. Our post on why TMV temperatures drift explains the fixes.

Should a failing TMV be repaired or replaced?

Most problems are fixed with a clean, descale, new seals or a new cartridge, then a recalibration and retest. Replacement makes sense when parts are no longer made, the valve keeps failing after proper servicing, or it isn't certified for the job it's doing, such as a TMV2 where TMV3 is needed. We'll show you the readings and tell you which we'd do. See repair or replace?

What should a TMV test record include?

The D 08 record sheet is a good template. It asks for:

  • the valve reference, location, outlet type and set temperature
  • supply temperatures and pressures
  • the mixed temperature at maximum flow
  • the fail-safe result (flow after 5 seconds, volume in 60 seconds)
  • the temperature after the cold is restored, and the drift since last time
  • whether strainers and isolation valves were clean and open
  • the final temperature and the next test date

Our asset registers keep all of that per valve.

Who is allowed to test and service TMVs?

HSG274 Part 2 says TMVs need regular routine maintenance by competent people, following the manufacturer's instructions. In practice that means someone trained on the valves, using calibrated thermometers, who understands the D 08 test and records it properly. In healthcare, your Water Safety Group and Authorising Engineer (Water) decide who's competent for your site.

TMVs and Legionella

Can TMVs increase the Legionella risk?

They can. The blended water after a TMV sits at a temperature Legionella likes, and HSG274 Part 2 and HTM 04-01 Part B both flag pipework downstream of TMVs as a concern. That's why the guidance asks for valves close to the outlet, short blended runs, no single valve feeding lots of little-used taps, and a risk assessment that weighs scalding against Legionella before a TMV goes in.

Do outlets with TMVs need regular flushing?

Yes, if they aren't used often. HSG274 Part 2 recommends flushing infrequently used outlets at least weekly. In healthcare, where outlets serving susceptible patients aren't in regular use, the risk assessment may call for twice weekly. Run the outlet long enough for the temperatures to stabilise and log it. Our post on TMVs after a quiet spell covers restarting buildings after closures.

Should we take out TMVs we don't need?

Possibly. HSG274 Part 2's maintenance table says to risk assess whether each TMV is required and remove it if not. HTM 04-01 Part B says thermostatic mixing devices should only be fitted where a risk assessment shows a need. A TMV on a staff-only kitchen sink, for example, may be adding Legionella risk without reducing much scald risk. Get your risk assessor's view before removing anything.
